This work documents three rural funerals in my Bouyei family. After three family members passed away within two years, I returned to my hometown in Guizhou, China, to photograph these rituals and explore ethnic culture, rural customs, death, and identity.
Between 2020 and 2022, I faced the successive deaths of my maternal grandparents and paternal grandfather.These losses filled me with fear and made me realize how close death is to each of us. We must learn to face it over and over, until we are no longer afraid. Photography became my way of learning to face death. This was one of the key inspirations behind the title Over and Over. The Chinese title of this work is 习以为常 (xí yǐ wéi cháng), a phrase that describes how something gradually comes to seem ordinary through repetition.
As a Bouyei person raised in a predominantly Han Chinese cultural environment, I have long felt a distance from my parents' rural hometown. Returning there for the funerals, I found the place both familiar and unfamiliar. I picked up my camera to record and observe everything around me. At first, I was unsure of my own position and perspective, photographing largely by instinct. As a family member, I had to help with the funeral arrangements and participate in the rituals while also photographing them. This gave me a range of experiences and perspectives.
By the second funeral, I had begun to consciously explore different viewpoints. After all three funerals, I came to an important realization: behind the Bouyei identity I had gradually lost sight of in everyday life, many cultural practices and ways of living continued to endure. In Bouyei villages, life connected to Bouyei culture continued as before, even when much of it no longer seemed remarkable. I gradually realized that picking up a camera had not made me an outsider to my family, because I had always been part of it. My existence, and my act of observing and documenting through photography, are themselves part of the contemporary experience of being Bouyei within a predominantly Han Chinese way of life. As a photographer, I observe this process while remaining part of it. Photography has brought me closer to things that were once familiar to me but that I had not necessarily understood. It has also made me realize that just because something no longer draws our attention does not mean it has lost its significance. This is the second layer of meaning behind the title Over and Over.
Another important purpose of this work is to show not only how a local culture approaches death, but also how an ethnic minority lives in the present. The Bouyei are neither frozen in the past nor defined by stereotypes; their lives and culture continue to evolve. Through documenting these funerals, I encountered many customs that remain alive, including Songshan, a funeral procession in which people form a long line to carry the deceased's coffin into the mountains for burial; lusheng funeral music, performed by local folk musicians who play farewell melodies to accompany the deceased on their final journey; and ritual lamentation performed by women. I also observed local beliefs associated with natural elements such as bamboo and trees.
This presentation is an edited selection from nearly 120 photographs. Beyond ethnic customs and funeral rituals, the complete work includes many images of everyday rural life during the funerals. Some show how people respond to death as though it were part of ordinary life. The local people are deeply familiar with death through their frequent participation in one another's celebrations and funerals. They help each other and work together to carry out the many tasks required for a funeral. Through these shared experiences, death becomes an ordinary part of their lives. This is another important source of the title Over and Over.
For me, photographing these familiar things gradually revealed aspects of them that I had not previously recognized. The experience deeply affected me and led me to reconsider my own culture.
