One evening, I was sitting in a café near a shopping mall. Looking out the window, my gaze suddenly met that of a beautiful woman printed on a huge billboard. In that moment, something about the strangeness of our eye contact released an intense feeling in me.
Images of these “beautiful women” exist almost everywhere within our field of vision, yet they usually go unnoticed. Most of the women depicted are unknown, anonymous. We cannot know for certain whether the person we see ever truly existed. They seem to form an immense underground population: the more visibly they are displayed in the real world, the more mysterious and unreal they become. In an age saturated with human images, when almost everything seems traceable to a source, their ubiquitous faces and the unknown behind them lingered in my mind like a fog. I began this project and started photographing them extensively.
Through repeated encounters, observations, and acts of capture, I began to feel as though both my camera and I were being watched by them. At times, their gazes placed me under a subtle pressure. In spaces where cameras are everywhere, we know that at any moment we may become an image ourselves. Yet as I used my camera to capture these women, the positions of viewer and viewed began to reverse. Every pair of eyes seemed to anticipate those approaching, or to follow those who had already passed before them. Their motionless figures and enduring gazes silently witnessed the moving phantoms of reality.
Most of these women appear in print as beautiful and self-assured. Retouched, displayed, consumed, and imagined, they are rarely required to possess an identifiable self. We neither do nor can ask who they really are. Conveniently, they cannot speak, and so they become vessels for our projections. Whether their images are celebrated, mocked, damaged, or abandoned, something strange happens: they continue to retain their beautiful faces and smiles.
Existing on flat surfaces, they can easily appear to lack depth or vitality. Yet through sustained looking, this very flatness began to produce a kind of hunger in me. These figures enter the image and then retreat from reality, leaving behind mysteries that cannot be verified. If their only contact with the world takes place through these flattened images, does that mean the encounter never truly happened? Does it mean that they do not exist?
Photography offered me no answers. I simply began collecting them, regarding each “her” as an individual while eventually seeing them together as a collective portrait. Gradually, they became vivid and full in my eyes. I had found a shelter for my hunger.
Eventually, I gave them a name: YoYo (幽幽).
In Chinese, the repetition of a character in a name carries a sense of intimacy. The character you (幽), however, evokes something secluded, dim, hidden, even ghostly. Like apparitions, these women flicker in the shadows, their quiet gazes shimmering from the darkness. YoYo is each one of them, and she is all of them at once.
Across these repeated encounters and searches, there were moments when meeting their eyes felt familiar, sometimes even sweet—before everything returned, finally, to ordinary estrangement.
